Life Insurance, Explained

No sales pitch, no fear tactics. Just the honest, plain-English version of what life insurance actually is, what it really costs, and why almost everything you assumed about it is probably wrong.

MS
By Marc Savolle · Licensed Health & Life Broker
About a 6-minute read

Let’s start with the thing nobody says out loud, because it changes how the whole subject feels.

The reframe

Life insurance has almost nothing to do with you. It’s about the people who’d be left holding the bill if you weren’t here.

That’s the whole idea. It’s not a bet on your own life or a morbid thing to think about — it’s a way of making sure that if the worst happened, the people who depend on you wouldn’t also get hit with a financial disaster on top of losing you. Mortgage, rent, kids, car payments, final expenses — life insurance is what keeps those from landing on someone you love.

So the real question isn’t “do I need it?”

Forget the generic advice. There’s really just one honest question:

If you died tomorrow, would anyone struggle financially?

A partner who couldn’t cover the rent alone? Kids who’d need to be raised? A co-signed loan someone else would inherit? Aging parents you help? If the answer is yes to any of that — that’s your answer. If truly no one relies on you financially and you have no debts anyone would inherit, you might not need much at all. Simple as that.

The surprise that stops most people cold

Here’s the aha moment that changes minds more than any other: life insurance costs a fraction of what people think. Study after study shows people overestimate the price by roughly three times or more. Folks picture a big monthly bill… and the real number is often shockingly small, especially if you’re young and healthy.

Why so cheap when you’re young? Because insurance is priced on risk, and a healthy young person is low-risk. Which leads to the single most counterintuitive truth in this whole world…

Counterintuitive but true

The best time to buy life insurance is when you feel like you need it least — young and healthy. That’s exactly when it’s cheapest, and you lock that rate in.

The only two types you need to understand

Ignore the jargon soup. It really comes down to two:

Most people

Term life

Covers you for a set stretch of time — say 10, 20, or 30 years. Cheap, simple, and it’s the right answer for most people. Think of it like coverage for the exact years your family needs you most: while the kids are growing and the mortgage is being paid off.

Specific needs

Whole life

Permanent coverage that never expires and builds a cash value over time. It costs more — sometimes a lot more — and makes sense for specific goals like final-expense planning or leaving a guaranteed inheritance. Powerful for the right situation, overkill for many.

An easy way to hold it: term is like renting coverage for the years you need it (cheaper, does the job), and whole life is like buying (permanent, builds value, costs more). Neither is “better” — they solve different problems.

“But how much do I even need?”

You don’t need a spreadsheet. A common starting point is enough to cover what you’d leave behind: replace a chunk of your income for the years your family would need it, plus wipe out big debts (mortgage, loans) and final expenses. Some people use a rough rule of thumb like 10× their annual income as a jumping-off point — but the honest answer is it depends on your life, and that’s exactly the kind of thing a broker figures out with you in a few minutes.

The myths that hold people back

Tap each one — the truth is usually the opposite of what you’d guess.

“I’m young and healthy, I don’t need it yet.”
Young and healthy is precisely when it’s cheapest — and buying then lets you lock in that low rate for decades. Wait until you “need” it and you’ll pay more, and a future health issue could make it far pricier or harder to get. The clock only works against you here.
“I have coverage through my job, so I’m set.”
Two catches. First, work coverage is usually smaller than you think — often just one or two times your salary, nowhere near enough to actually protect a family. Second, it typically disappears the day you leave the job. A policy you own goes with you no matter where you work.
“It’s too expensive.”
This is the big one — and it’s usually just a wrong guess. Most people overestimate the cost by about three times. For a healthy young adult, real coverage often runs about the price of a couple coffees a month. You won’t know your actual number until someone checks — and checking is free.
“I don’t have kids, so it’s pointless.”
Kids aren’t the only people who’d be affected. A spouse or partner on a shared lease or mortgage, a co-signed loan, aging parents you help, or even just final expenses — funerals routinely cost thousands. If anyone would inherit a bill or a gap, coverage still matters.
“Life insurance only pays out when you die.”
Not anymore. Many modern term policies come with living benefits — if you’re diagnosed with a serious illness like cancer or have a heart attack or stroke, you can access part of the benefit while you’re still alive to help with bills and treatment. It’s become a safety net for life, not just death.
